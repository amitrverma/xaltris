<div className="blog-essay-kicker">13.8 billion years in the making. Still a work in progress.</div>

<p className="fermat-lead-paragraph">I have been reading Simon Singh's <em>Big Bang</em> lately, and it has been one of those books that occasionally makes me put it down, stare into the distance, and marvel at what I have just learned. Not necessarily because the information is entirely new, but because something I have known for years suddenly acquires a different meaning.</p>

The other day, I found myself thinking about Carl Sagan's famous observation that we are made of star stuff. A beautiful thought, certainly. But the engineer in me couldn't resist a less romantic interpretation. Aren't we, technically speaking, nuclear byproducts? The remnants of stellar fusion reactions, scattered across the cosmos, recycled into planets, oceans, trees, and eventually people who spend their mornings wondering about such things?

The thought amused me. But it also set off a rather extraordinary chain of reflections.

> I am 13.8 billion years in the making.

Not quite in the literal sense, of course. I have been around for only a few decades. But the story of the matter that makes up my body stretches back almost to the beginning of the universe. Some of the hydrogen in me originated in the first few minutes after the Big Bang. Much of the carbon, oxygen, and other heavier elements were forged inside stars that lived and died long before our Sun existed.

And here I am, made from that ancient material, sitting on a tiny planet orbiting an unremarkable star, contemplating the history of the universe.

How wonderfully absurd is that?

<div className="fermat-section-label"><span>Cosmic history</span></div>

## A History That Never Had Me in Mind

About 13.8 billion years ago, the observable universe was in an extraordinarily hot, dense state. As it expanded and cooled, the first atomic nuclei formed. Hundreds of thousands of years later, neutral atoms could exist, and light began travelling freely through space.

Gravity gradually gathered matter into enormous structures. Stars ignited, galaxies formed, and nuclear fusion began producing many of the elements that would eventually make planets and life possible.

Generations of stars lived and died, enriching the cosmos with heavier elements. About 4.6 billion years ago, one particular cloud of gas and dust collapsed to form our Sun and the surrounding planetary system. A little later, Earth took shape.

For hundreds of millions of years, Earth was a place where chemistry unfolded without anyone around to appreciate it. Eventually, through processes we are still trying to understand fully, life emerged.

What followed was an extraordinary history of replication, mutation, natural selection, adaptation, and extinction. Life diversified into forms that would be almost unimaginable to us today. Entire ecosystems appeared and disappeared. Species flourished for millions of years before vanishing, leaving behind little more than fossils.

And through all of this, there was no indication that the universe was working towards producing human beings.

The dinosaurs did not exist so that mammals could eventually flourish. Mammals did not evolve so that primates could appear. And our ancestors did not survive countless generations with the objective of producing me.

They simply lived, reproduced, and passed on their biological inheritance. Those who failed to reproduce left no descendants. Those who succeeded became part of an unbroken chain extending through billions of years of life's history.

I happen to be one of its current outcomes.

There is something deeply humbling about that thought. But strangely, I don't find it diminishing. Quite the opposite.

<div className="fermat-section-label"><span>Hindsight</span></div>

## The Illusion of Inevitability

We humans have a peculiar relationship with randomness.

Once something has happened, we become remarkably good at explaining why it happened. We construct narratives, identify causes, connect events, and gradually convince ourselves that the outcome was somehow inevitable.

History is particularly susceptible to this. Looking backwards, the events that produced our present world often seem to follow a coherent trajectory. One discovery led to another. One civilisation gave way to the next. One invention made another possible.

But that coherence is largely a privilege of hindsight.

The future did not look nearly so orderly to the people living through those events. Countless other outcomes were possible, and many depended on circumstances nobody could have anticipated.

Our own existence is perhaps the most intimate illustration of this.

Consider the astonishing number of events that had to unfold in precisely the way they did for any particular individual to exist. Not merely the formation of Earth or the emergence of life, but every evolutionary divergence, every survival, every migration, every encounter between ancestors, stretching backwards through generations beyond counting.

Even within our parents' lifetimes, a small change in circumstances could have resulted in an entirely different person being born.

We sometimes describe this as beating impossible odds. I am not particularly fond of that interpretation. Probability is often misunderstood, especially when calculated retrospectively. The fact that an event has occurred does not mean it was destined to occur, nor does its apparent improbability make it miraculous.

> Something was going to happen. This is what happened.

There is also an important distinction between randomness and the absence of intention. The universe is not simply a chaotic lottery. Physical laws govern its behaviour. Evolution is shaped by natural selection, which is anything but random in its consequences. Yet these processes do not require a predetermined destination.

Rewind the history of Earth and let it unfold again, and we have no reason to assume that the same species, let alone the same individuals, would emerge.

The universe did not have my existence pencilled into its calendar.

And yet, here I am.

<div className="fermat-section-label"><span>Purpose</span></div>

## No Grand Plan Required

For much of human history, we have explained our existence through stories of creation, intention, and purpose. It is understandable why.

Our minds are exceptionally good at recognising patterns and attributing agency. We are accustomed to a world in which things happen because someone wanted them to happen. A house has a builder. A painting has an artist. A tool has a maker.

It is tempting to extend the same reasoning to the universe itself.

But the more I learn about the natural world, the less I feel the need for an intentional explanation of my own existence.

Science has revealed a universe of extraordinary complexity emerging through physical processes operating over immense stretches of time. It has shown how simple beginnings can produce astonishing complexity without anyone planning the final outcome.

There remain enormous unanswered questions, including why the universe exists at all and how life first emerged. I find those mysteries fascinating. But I see no reason to fill the gaps in our understanding with an assumed intention.

More importantly, I don't find the absence of a grand plan unsettling.

I don't need to believe that the universe was created for me, or that my arrival was part of some cosmic objective, to appreciate the extraordinary privilege of being alive.

In fact, I find the alternative far more fascinating.

I was never promised an existence. There was no guarantee that the circumstances necessary for my arrival would ever come together. No particular outcome was owed to me.

And yet, against this vast backdrop of cosmic and biological history, I get to experience a tiny fragment of it.

> Not merely exist as matter, but experience existence.

That distinction is extraordinary.

<div className="fermat-ornamental-divider" />

<div className="fermat-section-label"><span>Consciousness</span></div>

## The Universe, Experiencing Itself

Somewhere along the evolutionary journey, living organisms developed increasingly sophisticated ways of sensing and responding to their surroundings. Nervous systems emerged. Brains became more complex. Eventually, at least in our own lineage, consciousness acquired the remarkable capacity for self-reflection.

We do not yet fully understand how subjective experience arises from physical processes. Consciousness remains one of the most fascinating problems in science and philosophy.

But whatever its ultimate explanation, the result is astonishing.

Matter that once existed inside stars now participates in a system capable of wondering what stars are made of.

Atoms that have no awareness of their own existence are arranged into a human being who can contemplate the age of the universe, question his assumptions, appreciate a beautiful sunset, or laugh at a silly joke.

The universe, through one of its countless physical arrangements, has produced something capable of studying the universe itself.

I realise this sounds almost poetic. But the underlying reality is scientific.

And perhaps that is what I find most remarkable. We don't have to embellish the facts to make them extraordinary.

Consider what an ordinary day allows us to experience.

The pleasure of reading a good book. The exhilaration of a hard-fought badminton rally. A conversation that challenges something we have believed for decades. The satisfaction of understanding a difficult idea. The company of people we love. A moment of laughter over something completely inconsequential.

None of these experiences requires cosmic significance.

They are significant because we experience them.

The universe may be indifferent to whether I enjoy my morning coffee, but that doesn't make the enjoyment any less real.

Nor does the fact that my existence is temporary diminish the experience of being alive.

If anything, knowing that this particular arrangement of matter is fleeting makes the experience all the more remarkable.

<div className="fermat-section-label"><span>Curiosity</span></div>

## Still a Work in Progress

There is another aspect of this that I find particularly delightful.

After 13.8 billion years of cosmic history and billions of years of biological evolution, here I am, still discovering things that make me question what I thought I knew.

Recently, while revisiting some elementary astronomy, I found myself marvelling at the fact that the Sun occupies one focus of Earth's elliptical orbit rather than its centre.

I had learned about elliptical planetary orbits in school. I had studied conic sections and their foci. Both pieces of information had been sitting in my head for decades, yet somehow the connection had never quite registered.

When it finally did, I experienced that wonderful moment of surprise that accompanies a sudden change in understanding.

A tiny update to my mental model of the universe, decades after the original lessons.

It reminded me how much there is still to discover, and how easily familiarity can masquerade as understanding.

I spent my twenties immersed in technology, my thirties increasingly occupied with management and organisations, and my forties developing a deeper interest in human behaviour, history, evolution, and the sciences.

The more I learn, the more fascinating the world becomes. Not because I am getting closer to knowing everything, but because each discovery reveals how much remains beyond my understanding.

And I rather like that.

There is no finished version of me waiting somewhere in the future. No final state of understanding to achieve. No particular destination towards which all these years of living must converge.

I am simply a living organism with the unusual ability to examine its own existence, revise its understanding, and remain curious about what comes next.

A work in progress, in the most literal and delightful sense.

Not necessarily improving. Not necessarily progressing towards anything. Just changing, learning, experiencing, and occasionally being astonished by something I should probably have understood in high school.

<div className="fermat-section-label"><span>Being here</span></div>

## What a Magnificent Accident

It is easy to become accustomed to being alive.

We wake up, go about our routines, deal with responsibilities, pursue ambitions, worry about things that may never happen, and occasionally reflect on things that already have.

Existence becomes the background against which everything else unfolds. So familiar that we rarely stop to consider how extraordinary it is.

Perhaps that is inevitable. We are creatures adapted to surviving and navigating our immediate environments, not to spending every waking moment contemplating cosmic history.

But every now and then, a book, a conversation, or an unexpected connection between familiar ideas offers a different perspective.

For a moment, the ordinary becomes astonishing again.

I find myself thinking about the sheer length of the journey that preceded my arrival. The stars that had to form and die. The planet that had to emerge. The life that had to begin and persist. The countless generations of ancestors whose existence made mine possible.

None of it happened for my benefit.

And none of it guarantees anything about what comes next.

But here I am, alive in a universe that offers an almost inexhaustible supply of things to discover, people to know, experiences to enjoy, and ideas to explore.

I don't need to assign some grand purpose to this extraordinary chain of events. Nor do I need to convince myself that it was meant to happen.

It is enough to recognise that it did.

And to appreciate, perhaps a little more deeply than I did yesterday, the simple fact that I get to be here.

> I was never meant to be. Yet here I am. And what a magnificent accident it has been.
